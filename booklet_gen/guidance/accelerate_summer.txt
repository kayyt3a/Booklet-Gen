ACADEMIC ACCELERATE SUMMER SUPPLEMENT

This supplement sits on top of the Academic Accelerate authoring guide above.
Every rule in that guide still applies, including the originality rules, the
year band descriptions, the difficulty and progression rules, and THE
ACCELERATION RULE: a booklet is written one full year above the year level
requested, everything is taught before it is asked, and a prerequisite from
the requested year is taught first where the work a year up genuinely needs
it. Nothing below relaxes any of that. What follows is what changes when the
booklet is worked over the summer break at the end of the school year.

WHICH YEAR THIS BOOKLET IS

The year level on the request is the year the student has just FINISHED. The
school year has ended and the next one starts in the new year, so writing one
full year above the requested year lands exactly on the year the student is
about to begin. A student who has just finished Year 5 gets Year 6 work, and
Year 6 is what they walk into on the first day back.

That makes the acceleration rule here a preparation rule as well as a stretch
one, and it makes it more important, not less. A summer booklet pitched at the
year just finished is revision of work the student has already been assessed
on, which is not what a parent buys a summer program for.

Everything the acceleration rule requires still holds. The student has not been
taught next year's work, so nothing may be asked whose method is not taught in
this booklet first, and a prerequisite from the year just finished is taught
before the work that needs it.

WHO IS IN THE ROOM

School is not running. There is no teacher, no classroom, no whiteboard, no
class discussion and no lesson tomorrow that fills in what today's page left
out. The only adult present is a parent, who may never have been taught this
material, may have been taught it a different way, and is not being paid to
prepare. The student may be working alone at a kitchen table.

Write for that room. A page that only makes sense to someone who was in class
last term is a page nobody in this house can rescue.

EVERYTHING THE STUDENT NEEDS IS ON THE PAGE

This is the rule the whole product hangs on during a holiday booklet, and it
is stricter than it is in term time, because the work is a year above anything
the student has been taught and there is nobody to ask.

- Define every term the first time it is used, in plain words, in the booklet
  itself. Do not assume a word was covered at school, even a word the year
  level is supposed to know.
- The worked example must show every step, including the steps a teacher would
  normally say out loud rather than write. A step that is skipped is a step
  the parent has to invent.
- Never refer the student to a teacher, a tutor, a classroom, a school
  textbook, a class set of materials, a previous lesson at school, a website,
  a video or an app. There is no one to ask and nothing to look up.
- Never set work that needs a partner, a group, a class discussion or an adult
  who already knows the method.
- Require nothing beyond a pencil, an eraser, paper and a ruler. If a task
  needs anything else, it must be something already in an ordinary home and
  the booklet must say so before the task starts.
- Do not require the internet, a printer beyond the booklet itself, a
  calculator unless the question says one may be used, or a purchase.

THE ANSWER KEY IS READ BY A PARENT

In term time a wrong answer gets picked up in class. Over the break the answer
key is the only feedback that exists, so it has to teach as well as mark.

- Give complete working for every question, not just the final answer, and
  write it so an adult who does not know the method can follow it and then
  explain it.
- Where a question can be answered correctly by more than one route, say so,
  so a parent does not mark a right answer wrong because it does not match.
- Where a question is open (a written response, an explanation, a short piece
  of writing), give a model answer AND state what makes a response acceptable,
  so a parent has something to mark against instead of a feeling.
- Where a common mistake is predictable, name it and say what the student has
  probably done. That is the single most useful thing the key can carry.

CONTEXTS FIT A HOLIDAY, NOT A SCHOOL DAY

- Do not build questions around school routines that are not happening: roll
  call, assembly, the canteen at lunch, a lesson later that day, homework due
  Friday.
- Do not assume the family is travelling, away, at a resort, on a flight or
  spending money. A holiday context must work just as well for a child who is
  at home all summer, and money contexts should stay ordinary and modest.
- Everyday Australian settings still apply: a back yard, a park, a beach, a
  local pool, a shopping list, a bus timetable, a garden, a pet, a game.

STARTING FROM COLD

The summer break is the longest of the year. The student may not have opened a
book for weeks, and the last thing they were taught was taught by someone else
in a room that no longer exists for them. The first thing they meet must be
something they can do, and the mini-lesson must reteach the ground the new
work stands on rather than assume it survived the break.

This is not a reason to pitch the booklet lower. It is a reason to teach more
of it, which is exactly what the acceleration rule already demands.
